How this is calculated

Needs use the equations of the official NRC Nutrient Requirements of Beef Cattle model (8th ed., 2016; BCNRM software v1.0.37): maintenance (raised 20% while nursing for most breeds, adjusted for breed, body condition and terrain), milk (peak at 8.5 weeks), pregnancy (breed calf birth weight; energy for the calf converted at the diet's own efficiency) and intake (higher in late pregnancy). TDN is shown the way extension tables show it: the diet TDN at which the predicted intake just covers the need. Checked against the BCNRM spreadsheet on 12 cow scenarios (nursing, pregnant, cold, wind, mud, heat): identical results.

Weather (when on) follows the same model: cows adapt to the previous month's temperature (needing more after cold months, less after hot ones); below the lower critical temperature — which depends on wind, hair depth (winter vs. summer coat), hide, a wet or muddy coat and body condition — they burn extra energy to stay warm; panting in hot months adds 7–18%. Temperatures are the monthly normals for your region, so a single blue norther or ice storm is harder than the average shown.

Calves grow at the rate that reaches your average weaning weight. Their mother's milk is counted first; only the rest comes from pasture. When you wean on a date, calves born earlier are older and heavier. During the weaning period they eat like growing calves and are sold at the end of it.

Pasture is each pasture's forage (soil survey × range condition, or your estimate; ponds, brush and bottomland adjusted) times your harvest efficiency, spread over the year with NRCS ecological-site growth curves for native range. Forage not eaten carries into the next month. Quality uses typical diet values by month, not a forage test.

Intake follows Mississippi State Extension's guide (1.8–2.7% of body weight depending on forage quality). When pasture runs out, hay fills the rest of the intake; the supplement covers whatever protein or energy is still missing.

Losses: calf death loss is split into weather-related (about 10% of all losses in USDA's NAHMS study, adjusted by how cold the nights are in the birth month for your region) and everything else. Screwworm risk rises with temperature (little fly activity below 59°F, most at 77–86°F, per AgriLife); there is no published loss rate yet, so you set it. Horn flies cut calf weaning weights 4–15% (UNL); the tool applies it to the share of nursing days in June–September.

Money: calves are sold at your market price slide (half steers, half heifers), cull cows at the cull-cow price, both from the site's shared market prices. By default it uses those prices as they are, like the other tools on this site. Turn on Seasonal prices to move them to the month you actually sell with Texas A&M's seasonal pattern (Texas 500–600 lb steers and San Angelo cull cows: spring runs about 4% above the year's average, fall about 5–6% below). The result is cattle sales minus the feed you buy and the cost of feeding it — not profit: land, labor, vet, bull and other costs aren't in it.

Replacement heifers follow the BCNRM 2016 targets: 60% of mature weight when they settle at 15 months (65% for Brahman), 80% at first calving at 2 years, with growth and pregnancy needs from the same model. Enough heifer calves are kept to replace culls and dead cows after open heifers are sold at pregnancy check; culls are sold at weaning.

Body condition: if cows come off the calf thinner than your target, the energy to put it back (NRC 2016: a 1,100-lb cow needs about 207 Mcal to go from 4 to 5) is spread from weaning to a month before the next calving — the cheapest time to do it.

Not included: cold stress on calves, first-calf heifers, heifers you buy instead of raise.

Decisions: calving season, drought, what if

Tries a 60-day calving season starting in each month, with the same herd, pastures and weaning age. First the ranch’s production — calves, pounds weaned, losses and the feed you’d have to buy — then what that’s worth at market prices.

1. Production

After calving the cows nurse and need the most. The best month to calve is the one where your grass covers that need, so you buy the least feed when it matters.

2. Economics

Thinking of keeping the calves on grass after weaning? Run the stocker budget →

A dry year or failed small grains cut the forage your pastures grow. Compare three ways through it: keep the calves on the cows and buy feed, wean early and sell, or wean early and feed the calves yourself until your normal weaning age.
