Pastures

For rangeland, forage comes from the USDA-NRCS soil survey: its estimate of total plant growth per acre in a normal year, weighted by the soils inside each pasture. Improved pasture, wheat and hay meadows usually have no soil-survey number — enter your own estimate (your county agent can help).

How this is calculated

Needs use the equations in the NRC Nutrient Requirements of Beef Cattle (8th ed., 2016): maintenance (raised 20% while nursing for most breeds, adjusted for breed, body condition and terrain), milk (peak at 8.5 weeks), and pregnancy (calf birth weight 6.6% of the cow's weight, 365-day calving interval). TDN is shown the way extension tables show it: the pounds of TDN that meet the day's need at NRC-predicted intake. At the same inputs, the cow numbers match the University of Arkansas MP391 tables within 0.3%.

Calves need energy for maintenance and to reach the weaning weight you entered. Their mother's milk is counted first; only the rest has to come from pasture. The calves' shortfall is shown, but creep feed isn't priced in.

Pasture is each pasture's forage (soil survey or your estimate) times your harvest efficiency, spread over the year with NRCS ecological-site growth curves for native range. Improved pasture and small grains use typical growing seasons. Forage not eaten carries into the next month as standing forage. Quality uses typical diet values by month: dormant native range around 48% TDN and 4–5% CP in winter, growing grass near 60% TDN and 10–12% CP, wheat pasture over 20% CP. These are averages, not a forage test.

Intake follows Mississippi State Extension's guide: 1.8–2.5% of body weight depending on forage quality, and a bit more for nursing cows. When pasture runs out, hay fills the rest of the intake. Supplement is whatever is still needed for protein or energy, whichever takes more.

Not included: cold or heat stress, cows gaining back condition, first-calf heifers, replacement heifers, and year-to-year rainfall. In a drought year, use the lower forage numbers.
